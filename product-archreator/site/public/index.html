Software rarely fails because it was hard

It fails because the problem was misunderstood. A good answer to the wrong question, discovered late. AI made building faster without making that any less true — it just made it happen sooner.

THE FRAME

Nobody checks the question

You describe what you want, the agent builds something plausible, and the gap between them only shows up in what was delivered. There was never a step where the problem itself had to be complete.

THE HANDOVER

Design and delivery are separate worlds

Strategy decks don't build anything. Code doesn't explain itself. Whatever you meant changes shape at every handover — and with an agent, the handover happens on every prompt.

THE MEMORY

You explain it all again next time

New session, new agent, new contractor: the context lives in your head, so you retell it. What you actually built is scattered across chats nobody can read six months later.

Why this exists

Building with AI shouldn't cost you your own understanding

The fastest way to work with AI is to let it think for you. Take it, and you end up understanding your own business less than before you started.

archreator exists for the opposite outcome: you finish knowing more. The method makes you answer questions about your business you had been able to leave vague — and hands the answers to the agent as something it can actually build from.

That is the whole bet. Not faster output — output you can stand behind, from a business you now understand better than you did.

How it works

Six layers, four gates, one conversation

You talk. The agent asks, writes, and draws. You approve — or you don't, and it goes back a layer.

  1. 0Business designWho you serve, what hurts, what you offer
  2. 1StrategyWhat must become true, and what you must be able to do
  3. 2BusinessWho acts — including which parts the AI holds
  4. 3InformationWhat is known, and how sensitive it is
  5. 4ApplicationThe software that delivers it
  6. 5TechnologyWhat it runs on

The gates are the point

An architecture nobody approved is a document. archreator stops at four explicit moments and asks you, in plain language, to say yes. Your answer is written into the repository, so a year from now the record shows who decided what, and when.

Gate 0 · Business modelBefore anything is derived from it

You never need a terminal to approve. A reply in the conversation counts, and gets recorded.

What you get

A model that is also the build instructions

  1. You describe your business, badly at first

    The agent asks in business language, not architecture jargon — what people are trying to get done, what goes wrong today, what a win would look like. Most people discover here that they had named their customers wrong.

  2. It writes the model, and shows you the diagrams

    Markdown files in your repository, with diagrams above every table. Standard architecture concepts (ArchiMate) so the words mean the same thing to you, to your next developer, and to the agent.

  3. You approve, and it builds from what you approved

    Not a fresh guess from a prompt — the approved design is the input. Every element points at the file that makes it real, so you can check any claim in the model against the code in under a minute.

  4. The next change starts from what already exists

    A new requirement walks the same layers and finds them filled. The model gets more true over time instead of drifting — and you stop re-explaining your business to every new agent, developer, or partner.

Proof, not promises

The method is used on itself, in public

Everything below is in the repository right now. You can read the uncomfortable parts too — they are in the model on purpose.

  • →The project models its own organization. Customers, products, economics, capabilities — at full depth, published where anyone can disagree with it.
  • →It models its own method. Every skill and script appears as a component that names its file. If a claim were false, the build would fail.
  • →It records what it cannot do. Four of its seven stated outcomes have no way to be measured, and the model says so rather than quietly omitting them.
  • →Every approval is in the history. Who approved which gate, on what date, against which documents.
  • →Nothing phones home. The project holds no data about anyone who uses it. That is why its own adoption is unmeasured — a cost it chose.
  • →Checks run on every change. Every cross-reference in the model has to resolve, or the change does not merge.

Fair questions

The objections worth having

Isn't enterprise architecture a six-month programme?

It has been, which is why almost nobody outside large companies has one. The cost was always the expert's time. With an agent doing the writing, a small business can have a real model in an afternoon — and archreator lets you declare how deep to go, so a weekend app does not get a corporate treatment.

Do I need to know ArchiMate?

No. You need to answer questions about your own business. The agent handles the notation — and then teaches it back to you through the diagrams, which is the point: the standard vocabulary is what makes the model mean one thing to everyone who reads it.

Is this locked to one AI tool?

The method is markdown instructions. It ships today as a Claude Code plugin because that is what it was built in, but nothing about the method is specific to it. Your model is text in your repository — you can leave at any time and take all of it.

What does it cost?

Nothing. It is open source and stays that way. You pay whatever your own AI subscription costs, to your own provider — this project never sits in the middle.

I have no business — just an idea. Too early?

That is the best moment. The first thing the method does is make you state the business model out loud, which is exactly the work a founder keeps postponing until something expensive has been built on an assumption.
