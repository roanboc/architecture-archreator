# 3 — Modeling an organization that already knew its own processes

**Date:** 2026-08-14
**Kind:** client engagement
**Delivered:** not public

## What the method did not cover

**How far down to decompose.** The method said to model business processes and
capabilities but not where to stop. Applied uniformly, that produced more model
than the people who commissioned it would read.

**What an organization's process model looks like before we arrive.** The
organization already had macro processes in the classification used by its
quality-management system: strategic, operational, support and evaluation.
The useful level-1 model existed, although nobody called it architecture.

**Where the detail was worth having.** Pain was concentrated in one operational
macro process. The method had no way to distinguish a deliberately undetailed
branch from an unfinished one.

**What a capability map starts from.** Asking what an organization must be able
to do elicited org charts and current projects. The method supplied no starting
point against which stakeholders could recognize, reject and correct.

**Diagram labels.** With enough elements in one row, the stereotype was the
widest and least informative part of every node because the legend directly
above already explained the shape.

## What was done instead, and why

Processes and capabilities were modeled breadth first and depth on pain:
levels 1 and 2 were completed across the organization, while level 3 was
written only for the painful branch. Horizontal completeness made the map
trustworthy; selective vertical detail kept it readable.

The client's existing classification was adopted instead of replaced. A
capability map was drafted from an industry reference and presented as a
proposal: every item was confirmed, renamed or rejected aloud. This changed
the conversation from difficult recall to precise recognition without letting
the reference speak for the client.

Branches that would not be detailed were named explicitly. Stakeholders read
that as judgement rather than omission. Stereotypes were removed from content
diagram labels, while the mandatory legend retained the vocabulary.

## What generalized into the method

| Finding | Generalized rule |
| ------- | ---------------- |
| Processes and capabilities have levels | Model levels 1 and 2 across an organization; add level 3 only where a named pain justifies it |
| Quality management may already hold level 1 | Ask what has already been modeled before drawing a new macro-process map |
| Four macro categories expose missing work | Classify level-1 processes as strategic, operational, support or evaluation |
| Reference models make a better opening question | A reference proposes and never fills; the Requester confirms every surviving element |
| Undetailed branches looked unfinished | Use a focus table to record where decomposition stops and why |
| Repeated stereotypes obscured relationships | Keep stereotypes in notation legends, not content nodes |

These rules are now carried by `process-and-capability-levels`,
`architecture-document-style` and the discovery skills in `archreator`.

## What surprised us

The organization had already produced useful architecture without naming it
architecture. Its quality-management material was a better starting point
than discovery from nothing.

Naming branches that would not be detailed was received better than the detail
itself. Scoping down read as the difference between a consultant and a
transcript, not as work left incomplete.

## Confidentiality boundary

The client, industry, process names, pain and reference model are deliberately
absent. The note preserves the reusable shape of the engagement without facts
that could identify the organization.
