The first session

What to say, and what happens

  1. Say what you are, in one sentence

    "I run a small bakery with three staff and I want to take orders online." Or "I have an idea for a tool that helps teachers plan lessons." That is enough to start — the agent will decide whether you are modeling one application or a whole organization, and tell you which it picked.

  2. Answer questions about your business, not about software

    Who are they, what are they trying to get done, what goes wrong today, what would count as a win. Three to five at a time. If you do not know an answer, say so — "unknown" gets written down as an open question rather than guessed.

  3. Read the summary, and push back

    At each gate you get a compact summary with links to the exact documents. Disagreeing is the most valuable thing you can do here — a wrong customer segment caught at Gate 0 costs a paragraph; caught after delivery it costs the build.

  4. Approve, and let it build

    Say yes. The approval is recorded, and the agent implements from the design you just approved rather than from a fresh interpretation of your prompt.

Honest expectations

What this is and is not

It will slow down your first hour

Deliberately. Answering the questions is the work — it is the part that stops you from building the wrong thing quickly. From the second change onward it is faster than starting cold, because the model is already there.

It works without a paid AI plan

You get less thinking per session and shorter limits, so the same work takes more sittings. There is no hard blocker — the method is text, and any capable agent can read it.

You stay in charge of the business

The agent may not grant its own gate, decide what your business is, or change a principle you set. That is written into the method, not left to good manners.

You can leave with everything

Your model is markdown in your own repository. No export, no account to close, nothing held back. If you stop using archreator tomorrow, you keep the whole thing.

Go and try it on something you actually care about

A side project counts. A real business counts more — it is where the questions bite.
