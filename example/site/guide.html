Guide

How a requirement becomes an implemented, documented change — and how an AI system fits into that as more than the tool executing it.

1. Walk the EA layers, top-down

Every change is checked against five layers, in this order, before any code is written. A layer with nothing to say still gets an explicit "no change" — silence isn't a decision.

#LayerAnswers
1Strategy & MotivationWhy does this exist? Who cares? Does it hold up against the project's Principles?
2BusinessWho does what, through which service and process?
3InformationWhat information exists, how does it flow and persist?
4ApplicationWhich software services and components realize the business services?
5TechnologyWhat runtimes, hosting, and CI/CD does it run on?

Full process: ea-first-change · notation and grounding rules: ea-doc-style

2. Actors: human, AI, and hybrid

A Business Actor or Role no longer defaults to human by omission. Every actor states its kind on the stereotype line — Human, AI, or Hybrid (a person and an AI sharing one role) — and an AI or Hybrid actor carries three more things: how much it can do without a human, exactly what it's authorized to decide, and who it hands off to when it can't.

Autonomy levelMeaning
AdvisorySuggests; a human decides and acts
Co-pilotActs (drafts a complete change); a human reviews before it takes effect
Autonomous with checkpointActs independently; a human is notified and can intervene after the fact
Fully autonomousActs independently; no routine human checkpoint

Notation reference: ea-doc-style § Actors

3. Record why, not just what

A scope document explains an entire initiative's EA alignment. A decision record is smaller: one call, usually "why is this AI actor's autonomy set here and not somewhere else," recorded once, linked from the actor table row it explains, and never rewritten — a changed call gets a new record that supersedes the old one.

This project's own example: Decision 1 — Docs Agent autonomy level — why co-pilot was chosen over advisory-only or fully autonomous.
